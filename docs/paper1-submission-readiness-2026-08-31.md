## Required literature corrections

### 1. Animal-bite paragraph

**Charoenwisedsil et al. 2025 — verify and narrow the denominator.** The paper
supports the 2019–2024 Bangkok travel-clinic cohort, dogs at 46.3%, monkeys at
23.9%, and islands/beaches at 20.5%. These are selected post-exposure
consultations, not incidence among all travellers. Write: “one in five
consultations involved exposure on islands or beaches.”

- Charoenwisedsil R, et al. *Travel Medicine and Infectious Disease.*
  2025;66:102870. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tmaid.2025.102870),
  [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40516802/).

**Blaise et al. 2015 — use precise timing.** The study supports clustering at
Monkey Beach, Koh Phi Phi, and Lopburi Monkey Temple. Exposure timing was mean
day 10, median day 8, range 1–50. “Most occurred within the first ten days” is
defensible; “all” is not.

- Blaise A, et al. *Emerging Infectious Diseases.* 2015;21(8):1473–1476.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3201/eid2108.150346),
  [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4517731/).

**Juttuporn et al. 2020 — add the year limitation.** Foreign-tourist relative
risk was approximately 41 in 2016 and 2017. “About forty times” applies to those
two years and hospital-recorded injuries, not the whole five-year period.

- Juttuporn A, et al. *Chulalongkorn Medical Journal.* 2020;64(1):29–37.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.14456/clmj.2020.4),
  [publisher](https://he05.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/CMJ/article/view/175).

### 2. Rabies-delay paragraph

**de Feij et al. 2025 — verified, but worldwide rather than Thai.** Of 838 Dutch
travellers requiring PEP, 59.4% experienced delay; conflicting advice and
treatment unavailability were leading explanations. Present it as a worldwide
assistance-database cohort, not a Thai or Southeast Asian delay rate.

- de Feij M, et al. *Journal of Travel Medicine.* 2025;32(8):taaf111.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/jtm/taaf111),
  [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41123543/).

**Singkam et al. 2025 — correct an internal source inconsistency.** The study
supports 528 international patients and 53/528 (10.1%) initiating PEP at least
24 hours after exposure. Its abstract says 45.6% incomplete, while Results,
discussion, and Table 4 say 287/528 (54.4%) did not complete at the study centre
and 241/528 (45.6%) completed. No corrigendum was found.

Use the 10.1% delay figure. Either omit the completion percentage or disclose
the inconsistency and state that 54.4% did not complete at the study centre;
completion elsewhere was not necessarily observable.

- Singkam N, et al. *Travel Medicine and Infectious Disease.* 2025;67:102873.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tmaid.2025.102873),
  [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40602738/).

**Piyaphanee et al. 2012 — fix the denominator.** Sixty-six of 7,681 surveyed
travellers reported bites. Post-bite-practice data existed for 35; 13/35
(37.1%) sought hospital treatment. Do not describe 37.1% as the proportion of
all 66 bitten travellers.

- Piyaphanee W, et al. *PLoS Neglected Tropical Diseases.* 2012;6(9):e1852.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pntd.0001852),
  [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3459869/).

### 3. Thai health-system paragraph

**Replace the source for the exact 99% figure.** Tangcharoensathien et al. 2018
supports the three-scheme structure and the development of Thai UHC, but does
not directly state the exact “more than 99%” figure in the manuscript text.

Use the 2020 political-economy paper for the number: 68.2 of 68.9 million people,
more than 99%, covered by three schemes.

- Tangcharoensathien V, et al. *Bulletin of the World Health Organization.*
  2020;98(2):140–145.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2471/BLT.19.239343),
  [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6986227/).

The 2018 systems paper can remain for structure and history:

- Tangcharoensathien V, et al. *The Lancet.* 2018;391:1205–1223.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)30198-3),
  [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29397200/).

**State the catastrophic-spending threshold.** The financial-protection paper
supports a fall from 6.0% in 1996 to 2.0% in 2015 using the 10%-of-household-
consumption threshold. At the 25% threshold, the 2015 figure was 0.4%.

- Tangcharoensathien V, et al. *International Journal for Equity in Health.*
  2020;19:163. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-020-01273-6),
  [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7507254/).

### 4. Payment-friction paragraph

**Murphy et al. 2026 — change causal wording to association.** The systematic
review includes 25 studies and associates prior authorization with care delay
and adverse outcomes. Evidence is largely retrospective and US-based. It is
context for a cross-border guarantee-of-payment hypothesis, not direct evidence
from Thailand.

- Murphy J, et al. *American Journal of Medicine.* 2026;139(1):24–32.e1.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjmed.2025.08.018),
  [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40912445/).

**Himmelstein et al. 2020 — verified with context.** The current record supports
34.2% administrative costs in the United States versus 17.0% in Canada. These
are national administrative-spending estimates, not patient-level Thai delay.
The paper has a published erratum; use the current PubMed figures.

- Himmelstein DU, Campbell T, Woolhandler S. *Annals of Internal Medicine.*
  2020;172(2):134–142. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7326/M19-2818),
  [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31905376/),

### 5. Conceptual and methods framing

**Thaddeus and Maine 1994 — verified as an adaptation.** It decomposes delay
into deciding to seek care, reaching a facility, and receiving adequate care.
It supports localizing delay to separately measurable phases. Explicitly call
the SOSPHD interval model an adaptation; the original paper did not validate
this setting or these interval definitions.

- Thaddeus S, Maine D. *Social Science & Medicine.* 1994;38(8):1091–1110.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0277-9536(94)90226-7),
  [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8042057/).

**RECORD 2015 — trim the attribution.** RECORD supports that routinely collected
data are produced for operational/clinical purposes without an a priori research
question. It requires transparent reporting of missing-data handling, cleaning,
linkage quality, coding, validation, and bias.

It does not prescribe “quality assurance at the point of collection,” and it
does not by itself establish that missingness is this study's substantive
finding. Rephrase the manuscript to use RECORD for transparency and validation;
support prospective point-of-capture quality assurance with a separate source.

- Benchimol EI, et al. *PLoS Medicine.* 2015;12(10):e1001885.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1001885),
  [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4595218/).

## Seasonality decision — recommended treatment

Report observed monthly volumes, but label July 2019 and February 2019 as
registry-coverage gaps. Do not impute them as demand and do not use them as
evidence of a seasonal trough.

For any seasonal comparison:

1. show the complete observed series with the two months visibly flagged;
2. state that the data cannot distinguish zero activity from missing capture;
3. repeat the descriptive comparison with the flagged months excluded;
4. avoid formal seasonal-effect claims from this single-corridor, interrupted
   registry.

This preserves the data-quality result without discarding the high-season
pattern visible in adequately captured months.
